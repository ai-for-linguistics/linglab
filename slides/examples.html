Examples

Eight things to ask for

Plain language, in any order. Fully manual, fully AI, or a hybrid: you choose how much the assistant does.

These are examples, not limits: anything else you ask can be tried.

~30 s. A menu, not a script. Walk the eight tiles quickly, then take the slides one by one, or skip to the one you care about. Stress the last sentence: anything else you ask can be tried, including things nobody planned.
Menu 1 / 8

Look for principles

The assistant reads a paper and lists the principles it assumes, including the ones it does not advertise. It can also propose new ones, or compress facts into a few.

Try (click to copy)
~45 s. Principles are statements with code, so the same object is used for the paper's own assumptions and for new ideas. The assistant extracts each clause with its page and quote, flags the ones the paper does not state outright (auxiliary assumptions, choices of entailment or felicity), and can propose new principles two ways: within constraints you give, or by compressing a group of facts into fewer principles. You decide what enters the space.
Menu 2 / 8

Which judgment would matter most?

When two theories tie, the assistant finds a sentence on which they disagree and asks you for your judgment. Cheap to ask, informative to answer.

Try (click to copy)
~45 s. The most useful new data is the point where your best theories disagree. The assistant searches for a sentence or discourse on which two theories predict differently, writes it as a candidate fact, and asks you, as the informant, for a judgment with a confidence. It can also list the facts most worth reviewing first (contested or low confidence).
Menu 3 / 8

Compare theories

Put any two (or ten) theories side by side: where they differ on the facts, which principles explain the difference, and who wins on coverage against simplicity.

Try (click to copy)
~45 s. Because every theory is a set of principles and every cell is a recorded test, a comparison is a diff: the rows where the columns differ, and the principles that differ between the two bundles. The assistant states the difference in plain words, with the status of each cell (proof verifier, simulation, claim). The coverage-against-simplicity plot gives the global comparison; this is the local one.
Menu 4 / 8

Find what cannot go together

The assistant searches the space of principle sets for general results: incompatible sets, idle principles, principles that look like stipulations.

Try (click to copy)
~50 s. This is the part that goes beyond testing individual theories. Because principles are independent objects, you can ask about sets of them: this combination cannot pass these facts together (shown by a counter-model or a proof, labelled with its status); this principle changes nothing (idle); this one fixes a single fact and may just restate the data. Results are saved as findings with their evidence, so they are not lost in the chat. Searches over many combinations cost tokens and compute; the assistant states the cost before running them.
Menu 5 / 8

Change the page, or make a new report

The page is a file the assistant can edit. Ask for another view, a new plot, another format, or a completely different report.

Try (click to copy)
~45 s. The interface is not fixed. The report is one file that the assistant can change, with your copy kept in the project (the downloaded toolkit is never edited). Metrics, plots, tabs and the ladder are small named places the assistant edits on request. Other formats are generated from the same data, so they are always consistent with the page: slides, a LaTeX table, a plain-text summary. Whatever you change lives in your project and is yours.
Menu 6 / 8

Ask for ideas to revise a theory

When a theory fails, ask for the smallest fixes: add, drop or replace a principle, borrowed from another paper if that is the cheapest, each with what it gains and loses.

Try (click to copy)
~45 s. A failed cell is a starting point, not an end. The assistant looks at why it fails (the counter-model is shown, folded, in plain English), proposes a few minimal changes, and runs the whole fact set on each variant, so you see gains and losses at once. A fix is a new theory defined from its parent; today every new theory recomputes its cells. The assistant is told to flag a fix that only restates one fact: that is a stipulation, and cheap to spot with the compression view.
Menu 7 / 8

Check the assistant's work

You do not have to trust a formalization. Ask for a sheet that puts the paper's words, the formal code and a plain-English reading side by side, and for an independent reviewer.

Try (click to copy)
~50 s. This answers the question every linguist asks: how do I know the formalization says what the paper says. Each definition is shown with the quoted clause and page, the code, and a plain-English back-reading. The paper's own worked examples become tests that the formalization must reproduce before anything is audited. A separate reviewer who has not seen the formalizer's notes back-translates the code and lists mismatches. Where a typo is suspected, both a literal and a corrected version are kept, and the assistant tests whether the correction matters. All of this is relative to the formalization: it says nothing about the paper beyond what is checked.
Menu 8 / 8

Add data, papers and your own judgments

Drop in a paper and the assistant extracts its facts, with sources and confidence. Give it your own judgments, or ask it to elicit them, and each one becomes a row.

Try (click to copy)
~40 s. New material enters through the chat: a PDF in literature/, a list of sentences with your judgments, a case from a talk. Each datum becomes a fact with its source, a confidence, and an expected outcome, and the whole matrix is re-run for the new rows only. Related facts can be grouped, and you can ask the assistant to merge duplicates so that disagreements stay visible. Contested rows are shown with a dashed frame, and nothing is dropped silently.
